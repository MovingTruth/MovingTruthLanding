*A historical and evidentiary hypothesis*

![Moai at Rano Raraku, Easter Island. Photograph: Aurbina, 2004. Public domain.](/images/moai-rano-raraku.jpg)

## Premise

What if the most famous story of self-inflicted collapse in human history isn't true?

Easter Island — Rapa Nui — is taught almost everywhere as a warning. A society that grew too large, cut down its own forest to drag stone statues across the island, fought itself into famine and cannibalism, and collapsed before the Europeans ever arrived. A civilization that destroyed itself, entirely from the inside.

It is one of the most quoted cautionary tales of the modern environmental age.

It may also be substantially wrong.

There are two competing accounts of what happened on Rapa Nui. One says the island's own people caused their own ruin. The other says the island's people adapted successfully to one of the harshest environments on Earth — and were then destroyed by something that arrived from outside, roughly a century and a half ago, well within written memory.

**Both accounts are told here in full — not so you'll take this piece's word for which one is true, but so you can hold them side by side, see what each one asks you to believe, and think it through yourself.**

---

## 1. The Rise

Rapa Nui is the most isolated inhabited place on the planet — over 2,000 miles from the nearest continuous population, a single volcanic speck in the open Pacific. Polynesian voyagers reached it navigating by star, swell, and bird flight, with no instruments, sometime in the centuries before European contact. Scholars still argue the exact date — some evidence points as early as 800 CE, other evidence points closer to 1200 CE. Either way, a small founding population crossed open ocean with no knowledge of what they'd find, and found sixty-three square miles of nothing else for thousands of miles in any direction.

What they built there, alone, has no real parallel.

Nearly 900 moai — carved stone figures, some over 30 feet tall, some weighing more than 80 tons — were quarried from a single volcanic crater, carved with basalt tools, and moved miles across the island without wheels, without metal, without draft animals, to stand on ceremonial platforms called ahu. The current best explanation for how they were moved isn't dragging. It's walking — rocking the statue upright from side to side with ropes, the way you'd walk a refrigerator, an idea confirmed by experimental archaeology in 2012. It required rope-making, choreography, and a working social contract across a population that never numbered more than a few thousand.

This was not a society in decline. This was a society at the edge of what's humanly possible, achieving it in total isolation.

Something happened to it. The question is what.

---

## 2. The Official Story

*The first account. The one most people already know.*

The dominant version, popularized by Jared Diamond's 2005 book *Collapse*, goes like this: the islanders' own population growth outpaced the island's resources. Competing clans cut down the island's palm forest faster than it could regenerate — first for canoes and cropland, then, increasingly, to move ever-larger statues in a spiral of status competition. Without trees, there was no wood for canoes, which meant no deep-sea fishing, which meant famine. Famine produced warfare between clans. Warfare and starvation produced the toppling of the moai — many of which were later found deliberately pushed over — and, in some readings, cannibalism. By the time Europeans arrived in 1722, the population had allegedly already crashed from perhaps 15,000 to a few thousand.

**Ecocide.** A civilization that mistook its own ingenuity for permission, and paid for it.

It's a compelling story. It's also the version that turned Rapa Nui into a global metaphor — "the whole planet is Easter Island" appears in essentially every popular account of ecological overshoot written in the last twenty years, including Diamond's own closing argument.

That usefulness is not evidence that it's true.

## 3. What the Evidence Actually Shows

*The second account. Newer, less repeated, and harder to summarize in one sentence — which may be part of why it hasn't traveled as far.*

Beginning around 2006, archaeologists Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo published a body of research that challenges nearly every load-bearing claim in the ecocide account.

**The rats.** Polynesian voyaging canoes carried the Pacific rat, *Rattus exulans* — intentionally, as a food source, or as an unavoidable stowaway. On an island with no native land predators, rat populations can grow explosively. Hunt and Lipo's analysis of palm nut remains found extensive rodent gnaw marks, consistent with rats consuming seeds faster than the palm forest could reproduce. A forest can disappear without a single person cutting it down, if nothing is left behind to grow the next one.

**The soil.** Rapa Nui's volcanic soil is thin and dries quickly in ocean wind. Across the island, researchers have documented extensive fields of broken, fist-sized rock deliberately scattered over cropland — a technique called lithic mulching. The broken rock shades the soil, slows evaporation, and releases minerals as it weathers. This is not the signature of a people in desperate, disorganized decline. It's the signature of a people solving a hard agricultural problem with a technology suited to what they had.

**The bones.** If the ecocide account were correct, skeletal remains from the pre-contact period should show a rising signature of violence and malnutrition. The evidence for this is thin — far thinner than the popular narrative assumes. Large-scale warfare, in particular, is difficult to substantiate archaeologically before European contact.

**The population.** The commonly cited pre-collapse figure of 15,000 is an estimate, not a count, and a contested one. A stable population in the low thousands — smaller, adapted, sustainable — fits the archaeological footprint at least as well as a boom-and-bust curve of 15,000 down to 3,000.

None of this proves the island was a paradise with no hardship. It proposes something narrower and more specific: that the moai-building society was not, at the moment Europeans arrived, in the middle of collapsing.

## 4. What Actually Happened After 1722

The Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen landed on Easter Sunday, 1722 — which is where the island's European name comes from. Within hours of first contact, his sailors shot and killed several islanders. That was the opening exchange.

Subsequent European visits over the following century — Spanish in 1770, James Cook in 1774, La Pérouse in 1786 — brought the usual freight of first contact: introduced disease, against which an isolated population had no accumulated immunity.

Then, in 1862 and 1863, Peruvian slave ships — "blackbirders" — raided the island directly. They took an estimated 1,500 people, close to half of everyone left alive, to work the guano islands and plantations of Peru. The captives included Rapa Nui's king, Kaimakoi, and most of the island's remaining *rongorongo* readers — the priestly, literate class. The vast majority died within one to two years under conditions that amounted to slow killing.

International diplomatic pressure eventually forced the return of the survivors. Roughly a hundred made it back alive. The ship carrying them also carried smallpox. It spread through a population already reduced to a fraction of its former size. By the 1870s, the number of Rapa Nui people left on the island had fallen to somewhere near 110.

A Scottish sheep-ranching company then leased most of the island, fenced the surviving population into a single village — Hanga Roa — and ran sheep across everywhere else for decades. Chile formally annexed the island in 1888, by which point there was barely anyone left to annex it from.

**This is the collapse that is documented, dated, and named. It happened after contact, not before it. It took a decade and a half, not centuries. And it was done to the island, not by it.**

---

## 5. The Orphaned Script

Rongorongo is a system of glyphs carved into wooden tablets, found nowhere else on Earth, and — as far as current scholarship can determine — one of only a small handful of times in all of human history that writing was independently invented rather than borrowed or adapted from an existing script.

No one alive can read it.

Not because the writing is lost. Dozens of tablets survive, in museums and collections around the world. The symbols are sitting in front of us, photographed, catalogued, studied for over a century.

What's missing isn't the text. It's the readers. The people trained to interpret rongorongo were overwhelmingly members of the priestly class — precisely the people the 1862 slave raids targeted and removed. Within a single generation, a script that had been alive was orphaned. The tablets remained. The living tradition that made them legible did not survive the decade that followed.

A civilization that invented writing, on its own, in complete isolation — and then had the people who could read it taken away on a ship, fast enough that no one had time to write down what the symbols meant before it was too late.

That is not a metaphor. That is what happened.

---

## 6. Why the Self-Destruction Story Persists

The ecocide narrative isn't presented here as invented out of nothing. Deforestation happened. Hardship happened. The debate among archaeologists over exactly how much of each cause did how much damage is real, ongoing, and not fully settled — this theory doesn't claim otherwise.

But notice what work the popular version does, regardless of how the scholarly argument eventually resolves. It locates the catastrophe *inside* Rapa Nui society — their overreach, their competition, their mismanagement — years or centuries before a single European ship arrived. It makes the arrival of slavers and smallpox a footnote to a story that was, in this telling, already finished.

A story where a civilization destroys itself is a story that asks nothing further of anyone else.

A story where a civilization adapts, survives, and is then dismantled by disease and forced labor in the span of fifteen years is a very different kind of story — and a much less comfortable one to have turned into the world's favorite parable about ecological hubris.

This is not a claim that the ecocide narrative was constructed deliberately to hide anything. It's an observation about which version of events was easier to teach, easier to repeat, and easier to live with — and a question about how many other "self-destroyed" civilizations might deserve the same second look.

---

## 7. What Would Settle This

A theory that can't be tested against evidence isn't a theory. It's a preference.

If the self-destruction account is closer to correct, the archaeological record should show, before 1722: rising rates of skeletal trauma consistent with warfare, clear nutritional stress markers, and a population curve that peaks and crashes independent of any European ship's arrival.

If the displaced-collapse account is closer to correct, the record should show a population that was relatively stable and adapted at the moment of contact, a demographic and cultural collapse that tracks tightly with the 1862–1877 window, and a loss of specific cultural knowledge — like rongorongo literacy — that maps onto exactly who was taken, not onto a slow, general decline.

The second pattern is the one the current evidence increasingly shows. That could change with the next excavation. It should be allowed to.

## 8. The Central Hypothesis

> **Rapa Nui's moai-building society was not, at the point of European contact in 1722, in the midst of a self-inflicted ecological collapse. It was an adapted, functioning population that was subsequently destroyed — by introduced disease and, decisively, by the 1862–1863 slave raids that removed roughly half the remaining population, including the class of people able to read the island's own writing system. The dominant "ecocide" narrative, whatever partial truth it contains, has been amplified far beyond what the evidence supports because a story of self-destruction is more comfortable to tell than a story of what was actually done, and to whom.**

This is not a claim that every archaeologist agrees. Many still don't. The debate between the Diamond account and the Hunt-and-Lipo account is active, contested, and genuinely unresolved in places.

What this theory asks is narrower: not *believe this version instead*, but *notice how much easier the first version was to believe, and ask why.*

---

That question doesn't stop at Rapa Nui.

Somewhere behind almost everything you already believe about how a people, a nation, or a civilization fell, there is a version that won. Not always because it was the only one told. Not always because it was the true one. Because it was the version that got repeated — in the textbook, the documentary, the offhand line a teacher said once and never had to defend.

You did not choose which one you were handed. Nobody asks a child which account of Rome, or the Maya, or any fallen empire they'd like to grow up believing. The story arrives first, already finished, wearing the full authority of fact — and by the time you're old enough to ask who wrote it, it has already done its work. It has already told you something about what people are, and what they deserve, and whether ruin is a thing that happens to you or a thing you bring on yourself.

So ask it here, and then ask it everywhere else this pattern might be hiding: **who benefits from the version you were handed — and what would you have to be willing to feel, to let it be the other one instead?**

The moai were never carved facing out to sea, watching for danger from the horizon. They face inland, toward the villages, toward the people who made them. They were built to watch over the living.

**Whatever finally destroyed the people who carved them, it didn't come from the direction the statues were looking.**

---

There's a version of this that hasn't finished happening yet, and it wouldn't need a slave ship.

Rongorongo didn't vanish because anyone burned it. The tablets survived. What vanished was the small number of people able to read them — removed within a single generation, with no copy of that knowledge that didn't depend on them staying alive.

Most of what makes up a life now — the photos, the messages, the work, the record of a self — sits somewhere its owner doesn't actually control: servers, accounts, platforms, terms of service nobody reads, run by systems a shrinking number of people understand end to end. Not carved in stone. Not archived by the person who made it. Reachable only through infrastructure owned by somebody else.

It wouldn't take a war to erase most of it. It would take a policy change, a bankruptcy, an acquisition, or one person with the right permissions and the wrong day.

The moai have stood in stone for nine hundred years, facing the people who made them, whether or not anyone alive still remembers what they meant. **Ask what you're building your own memory out of — and who, right now, is holding delete.**
