Ask Sheldon Cooper how many colors are used across the world's flags, and he will not answer the question. He will ask you, first, to define your terms.

He'd be right to.

---

Vexillologists — people who study flags for a living — generally recognize ten core colors: red, blue, white, green, yellow, black, orange, brown, gray, purple. Some drop gray, because it shows up so rarely, and call it nine.

Ten, or nine, depending on how much you care about gray.

---

But that count only works because of a small, deliberate mercy: gold gets folded into yellow. Light blue gets folded into blue. Pink gets folded into red.

Stop folding, and count only what the eye actually sees, and the same flags produce thirteen distinct colors instead of ten.

Nothing about the flags changed. Only the categories drawn around them.

---

Orange shows up on about nine flags, total, out of nearly two hundred countries. Purple on fewer still — historically the most expensive pigment ever made, too costly for most nations to claim as an identity.

Here is the rest of it. The count depends on how far you're willing to split.

Folded down to ten:

- Red — ~76%
- White — ~72%
- Blue — ~52%
- Green — ~45%
- Yellow — ~45%
- Black — ~31%
- Orange — 4–5%
- Brown — 3–4%
- Gray — 2–3%
- Purple — 1–2%

Split back out to thirteen:

- Red
- Pink
- Blue
- Light Blue
- White — ~72%
- Green — ~45%
- Yellow
- Gold
- Black — ~31%
- Orange — 4–5%
- Brown — 3–4%
- Gray — 2–3%
- Purple — 1–2%

No percentage sits next to Pink, Light Blue, or Gold. Nobody has ever published one. The ten-color number is the only number that exists for any of the three — split on the list, still fused in the data.

---

Go looking yourself, though, and two of the three gaps aren't actually empty.

Light blue, on its own — Argentina, Botswana, San Marino, Somalia, Kazakhstan, and a handful more, depending on exactly where you decide "light blue" stops being "blue" — lands somewhere around five to ten flags. Call it five percent, loosely.

Pink is smaller still. The number most sources agree on is two: Spain and Mexico, and even then only as a few square inches of a lion or an eagle buried inside a coat of arms, not a color either country chose on purpose. Something closer to one percent — of a color nobody was actually trying to put there.

Gold is the only one of the three that stays empty no matter how hard you look. Nobody has ever gone flag by flag and separated it from yellow. Not because it's rare — gold is one of the most common colors on Earth's flags — but because nobody ever thought to ask the question the way they'd already stopped asking it about pink and light blue.

---

Jamaica is the one flag on Earth with none of the three colors nearly every other flag reaches for. No red. No white. No blue. Only black, gold, and green — strength, sunshine, and a fertile land. *Hardships there are, but the land is green and the sun shineth.*

Every other sovereign nation on the planet chose from the same overwhelming instinct. Jamaica didn't.

---

Every flag, everywhere, is designed to fly with its pole — the hoist — on the left. That's not an accident of habit. It's the position heraldry called dexter, the side of honor, for a thousand years before anyone put a flag on a rope.

Walk around to the back of it, and the hoist is on the right. Whatever was written or drawn on the front is now backwards — mirrored — unless someone built an entirely separate reverse side on purpose, which almost nobody does.

Same flag. Same cloth. Only the side you're standing on changed.

---

Leonardo da Vinci wrote almost everything in his own notebooks the same way — right to left, every letter reversed, legible only if you hold the page up to a mirror. The notes beside his Vitruvian Man are written exactly like that. The most famous drawing of human proportion in the Western world sits next to a paragraph nobody could read normally without turning it around first.

I think about that page more than I probably should. A man drawn to prove there's an ideal, exact, countable relationship between the length of your arm and the width of your shoulders — right next to a sentence that only tells the truth backwards.

---

Somewhere between nine and thirteen is the real number of colors on the world's flags. Somewhere between the front of a flag and the back of it is the real image. Neither answer is wrong. They just depend on which side you're standing on, and how much you were willing to define your terms before you asked the question.

The flags never move. Only the ones counting them do.
