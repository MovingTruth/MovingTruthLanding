You know this one.

A magnifying glass, a piece of paper or a dry leaf, a lucky patch of sun. You angle the glass until the bright dot on the leaf shrinks down to something small and hard and still, and you hold it there, and it starts to smoke, and then — with nothing but glass and sunlight and a steady hand — it's fire.

A lens doesn't create anything. It only gathers light that was already scattered everywhere, all at once, and bends it down to a single point. That's the whole trick. Concentration.

The James Webb Space Telescope does the same trick. Just far, far better than a magnifying glass ever could.

---

There is a rule it has never broken, not once, not for a second, since the day it opened its mirror in space.

It is not allowed to look at anything bright.

---

Its sunshield is the size of a tennis court — five layers, each thinner than a human hair, unfolded a million miles from Earth, angled so precisely that the sun, the Earth, and the moon stay permanently locked behind it. Mission planners call that space behind the shield a keep-out zone. Nothing bright is ever allowed to cross into it. If it did, instruments cooled by a decade of engineering to nearly 370 degrees below zero would flood with heat and go blind in the one part of the spectrum they exist to see.

So the telescope was built around a single, absolute posture: face away. Always. Everything it was ever meant to do depends on never once turning around.

---

What it faces instead is the oldest light there is.

Light that left its source before the sun existed. Before the Earth existed. Some of it left its source before most of the elements in your body had finished forming inside a star. That light has been traveling, alone, in the dark, for over thirteen billion years, getting fainter with every mile — until eighteen hexagonal mirrors, gold-coated, beryllium, cooled past reason, catch the last of it and do the one thing that's never been done to it before: notice it.

Face away from everything bright enough to be seen easily. Face toward the only things too faint and too old for anyone to have ever managed to see at all.

---

Here is the part almost nobody talks about.

The sun it can never face is also the thing moving it forward.

Photons don't weigh anything, but they carry momentum, and a shield the size of a tennis court catches a lot of them. Every second, in total silence, sunlight presses against the back of it and nudges the telescope's orbit — a little, in a direction engineers have to keep correcting for. Every few weeks, its thrusters fire. Not to go anywhere new. Just to cancel out how far the sun has quietly pushed it since the last correction.

It is being moved, permanently, gently, by the one thing it can never turn around to see.

---

That correction only works as long as there's fuel left to make it.

The telescope launched with enough for a ten-year mission. The launch itself was so precise — so little fuel wasted getting it into position — that NASA revised the estimate upward. Twenty years. Maybe more.

And then, someday, less precise than any of that: none.

---

Nobody is planning to refuel it. There's no servicing mission on the books, the way there was, five separate times, for Hubble. When the fuel runs out, the corrections stop. The keep-out zone stops being a maintained boundary and starts being whatever happens to an object left alone, drifting, for the rest of the time it has, in an orbit nobody is steering anymore.

Nobody knows, with any precision, where it ends up after that. Only that it does. That the machine which spent its entire working life turning its face from anything bright enough to blind it will, eventually, belong to nobody — and point wherever the drift takes it.

---

So here is the question underneath all of it.

What if the rule was never really *never face the light* — what if the rule was always *never face the light, for as long as someone is still correcting for it*?

What if the whole architecture of the thing — the shield, the mirror, the keep-out zone, the thirteen-billion-year-old photons it exists to catch — was never a permanent posture at all, but a very long, very expensive way of buying time?

What if, somewhere past the last correction burn, drifting in the dark with nobody watching, the instrument built entirely to never do what a magnifying glass does — finally does it anyway?

---

Not allowed. Just, it turns out, for as long as someone keeps paying for the fuel.
